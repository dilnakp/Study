Area & Volume from Vectors

Parallelogram with sides $a, b$ and angle $\theta$ has area $ab\sin\theta = |\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}|$. Adding third edge $\mathbf{c}$ gives height $|\mathbf{c}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}|$ where $\hat{\mathbf{n}} = (\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b})/|\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}|$:

$$V = |\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}||\mathbf{c}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}| = |\mathbf{a}\cdot(\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c})|$$

looks_two Page 3

Full Turn & Integral Multiples

A full turn is $2\pi$. If $\theta$ is the smallest rotation mapping a lattice to itself after $n$ steps:

$$n\theta = 2\pi \implies \theta = \frac{2\pi}{n}$$

where $n$ is a positive integer.

looks_3 Page 4

Sharing a Quantity Among Cells

A quantity held by $k$ identical cells is counted as $1/k$ for each:

• Corner point (touches 8 cubes): $\mathbf{1/8}$
• Face point (touches 2 cubes): $\mathbf{1/2}$
• Interior point (1 cube alone): $\mathbf{1}$

looks_4 Page 7

Dot Product & Direction Angle

From $\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = |\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|\cos\theta = \sum u_i v_i$, two cubic directions $[hkl]$ and $[h'k'l']$ satisfy:

$$\cos\theta = \frac{hh' + kk' + ll'}{\sqrt{h^2+k^2+l^2}\sqrt{h'^2+k'^2+l'^2}}$$

looks_5 Page 8

Normal & Distance of a Plane

For plane $px+qy+rz=s$, normal is $(p,q,r)$ and distance from origin is $|s|/\sqrt{p^2+q^2+r^2}$. For intercept plane $hx/a + ky/b + lz/c = 1$:

$$d = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(h/a)^2 + (k/b)^2 + (l/c)^2}}$$

looks_6 Page 9

Volume of a Sphere & Packing

Sphere volume is $\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$. With $N$ atoms per cell of volume $V_{\text{cell}}$:

$$f = \frac{N\cdot\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3}{V_{\text{cell}}}$$

For SC ($N=1, a=2r$): $f = \pi/6 \approx 0.52$.

filter_7 Page 11

Interference Condition

Constructive interference occurs when path difference $= n\lambda$. Rays reflecting from adjacent planes of spacing $d$ at glancing angle $\theta$ have:

$$2d\sin\theta = n\lambda$$

filter_8 Page 12

Dot Product & Perpendicularity

Since $\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c} \perp \mathbf{b}, \mathbf{c}$, defining $\mathbf{a}^* = \mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c}/V$ gives:

$$\mathbf{a}^*\cdot\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{a}^*\cdot\mathbf{c} = 0$$

$$\mathbf{a}^*\cdot\mathbf{a} = \frac{\mathbf{a}\cdot(\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c})}{V} = 1$$

filter_9 Page 15

Perpendicular Bisector

Points $\mathbf{k}$ equidistant from $\mathbf{0}$ and $\mathbf{G}$ obey $|\mathbf{k}|^2 = |\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{G}|^2$, which simplifies to the normal bisector of $\mathbf{G}$:

$$2\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{G} = G^2$$

grid_4x4 Section 1: Crystal Lattice, Unit Cell and Basis (1.1–1.4)

Puri and Babbar pp. 1–5 • Point lattice, translation vectors (Eqs. 1.1–1.2), primitive vs. non-primitive cells, Wigner–Seitz cell & basis.

grain 1.1 Definition of a Lattice & 1.2 Translation Vectors

A point lattice (space lattice) is an infinite array of imaginary points in 3D space in which every point has identical surroundings. This holds only if the points form a repeating pattern moved by a translation operation:

$$\mathbf{T} = n_1\mathbf{a} + n_2\mathbf{b} + n_3\mathbf{c} \quad \text{(1.1)}$$ $$\mathbf{r}' = \mathbf{r} + \mathbf{T} = \mathbf{r} + n_1\mathbf{a} + n_2\mathbf{b} + n_3\mathbf{c} \quad \text{(1.2)}$$

where $n_1, n_2, n_3$ are arbitrary integers and $\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}, \mathbf{c}$ are the fundamental translation vectors. A perfect lattice satisfies Eq. (1.2) for every integer choice; an imperfect lattice does not admit such vectors.

Result 1 — Primitive vs. Non-Primitive Vectors (Fig. 1.1):
Vectors whose translation operation has integral coefficients are primitive ($\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{b}_1$). Vectors that need non-integral coefficients, for example: $$\mathbf{R}' = \mathbf{R} + \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{a}_2 + \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{b}_2$$ are non-primitive ($\mathbf{a}_2, \mathbf{b}_2$). The book prefers an orthogonal, shortest set.

select_all 1.3 Unit Cell, Wigner–Seitz Cell & 1.4 Basis

  • Unit Cell: A parallelogram (2D) or parallelepiped (3D) built from the translation vectors. Repetition of its translation generates the full lattice.
  • Primitive (P) vs. Non-Primitive (NP) Cell (Fig. 1.2): A cell is primitive when its lattice points lie at its corners only, so its effective count is 1. A non-primitive cell has points inside or on its faces, so its effective count is greater than 1.
  • Wigner–Seitz Cell (Fig. 1.3): Constructed by drawing the perpendicular bisectors of the lines from a lattice point to its neighbours; the smallest enclosed volume is always primitive.
  • Conventional Unit Cell: Chosen for convenience; it is the primitive cell of least volume, unless a non-primitive cell shows higher symmetry.
  • 1.4 Basis and Crystal Structure (Fig. 1.4): A basis is an atom or group of atoms placed identically at every lattice point: $$\text{Space Lattice} + \text{Basis} \longrightarrow \text{Crystal Structure}$$ A lattice is mathematical; a crystal structure is physical. Fourteen 3D lattices suffice because the bases vary.

rotate_right Section 2: Symmetry Operations, Point Groups & Space Groups (1.5–1.6)

Puri and Babbar pp. 6–9 • Four basic operations, proof excluding 5-fold rotation (Eqs. 1.3–1.4), reflections, inversion, point & space groups.

sync 1.5 Symmetry Operations & 1.6 Point and Space Groups

The four basic symmetry operations are:

  1. Translation $\mathbf{r} \to \mathbf{r} + \mathbf{T}$: Defined by Eq. (1.1); applies to lattices only.
  2. Rotation by $\theta$ about a point (2D) or axis (3D): Since the lattice repeats after a full turn ($2\pi$), the multiplicity $n$ satisfies: $$n\theta = 2\pi \quad \text{or} \quad \theta = \frac{2\pi}{n} \quad \text{(1.3)}$$ Compatibility with translations restricts $n$ to $1, 2, 3, 4,$ and $6$ (Fig. 1.5). Five-fold rotation ($n=5$) is excluded (Figs. 1.6, 1.7).
  3. Reflection in a plane $m$ (or line in 2D): Divides the lattice into mirror-image halves (Fig. 1.8).
  4. Inversion $\mathbf{r} \to -\mathbf{r}$ about a centre $\bar{1}$: Exists in 3D only.

Point Groups & Space Groups (1.5.2–1.6):

  • Combining each of the 5 allowed rotations ($1, 2, 3, 4, 6$) with or without a mirror gives the 10 two-dimensional point groups (Fig. 1.9): $$1,\ 1m,\ 2,\ 2mm,\ 3,\ 3m,\ 4,\ 4mm,\ 6,\ 6mm$$
  • The 3D case adds inversion $\bar{1}$ and its combinations, giving 32 three-dimensional point groups.
  • A point group is the set of point operations that leave one point of the crystal fixed (symmetry of lattice + basis). The full set of symmetry elements, including translations, is the space group.
Result 2 — Counts (Page 4):
• Two dimensions: $10$ point groups, $5$ Bravais lattices, $17$ space groups.
• Three dimensions: $32$ point groups, $14$ Bravais lattices, $230$ space groups.

waves 2.1–2.2 X-Ray Diffraction: Bragg's Law & Laue's Equations

X-rays have wavelengths too short for ordinary optical gratings. Von Laue (1912) proposed a single crystal as a 3D grating, confirmed by Friedrich and Knipping with zinc blende ($\text{ZnS}$). Crystal electrons oscillate at the incident beam frequency and scatter secondary waves that interfere (Fig. 2.1).

  • 2.2.1 Bragg's Treatment (Fig. 2.2): Parallel atomic planes with spacing $d$ and glancing angle $\theta$ have path difference $2d\sin\theta$, giving reinforcement when:
    $$2d\sin\theta = n\lambda \quad \text{(2.1)} \qquad (\sin\theta \le 1 \implies \lambda \le 2d)$$
  • 2.2.2 Laue's Treatment (Figs. 2.3, 2.4): Two scatterers A and B separated by $\mathbf{r}$ with incident unit vector $\hat{\mathbf{n}}_1$ and scattered $\hat{\mathbf{n}}_2$ (angle $2\theta$ between them) have scattering normal $\mathbf{N} = \hat{\mathbf{n}}_1 - \hat{\mathbf{n}}_2$ with $|\mathbf{N}| = 2\sin\theta$ and phase:
    $$\phi = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{N}) \quad \text{(2.2)}$$ $$\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{N}) = 2\pi nh', \quad \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(\mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{N}) = 2\pi nk', \quad \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(\mathbf{c}\cdot\mathbf{N}) = 2\pi nl' \quad \text{(2.3)}$$ $$2a\sin\theta\cos\alpha = h'\lambda = nh\lambda$$ $$2b\sin\theta\cos\beta = k'\lambda = nk\lambda \quad \text{(2.4)}$$ $$2c\sin\theta\cos\gamma = l'\lambda = nl\lambda$$ $$\cos^2\alpha + \cos^2\beta + \cos^2\gamma = 1 \quad \text{(2.5)}$$
    The direction cosines of $\mathbf{N}$ are proportional to $h/a, k/b, l/c$, so $\mathbf{N}$ is normal to $(hkl)$. Using Eq. (1.7) in Eq. (2.4) gives $2d_{hkl}\sin\theta = n\lambda$ (Bragg's law). Convention: $n$ is absorbed into $(nh\ nk\ nl)$ as a first-order ($n=1$) reflection.

hub 2.4–2.7 Reciprocal Lattice, Ewald Sphere & Brillouin Zones

Each set of parallel planes $(hkl)$ is represented by a normal vector of length $1/d_{hkl}$ (Eq. 2.10), giving the reciprocal basis in the crystallographic ($1/d$, Eq. 2.13) and solid-state ($2\pi/d$, Eq. 2.18) conventions:

$$\mathbf{a}_{hkl} = \frac{1}{d_{hkl}}\hat{\mathbf{n}} \quad \text{(2.10)}$$ $$\mathbf{a}^* = \frac{\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c}}{V}, \quad \mathbf{b}^* = \frac{\mathbf{c}\times\mathbf{a}}{V}, \quad \mathbf{c}^* = \frac{\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}}{V} \quad \text{(2.13)}$$ $$\mathbf{a}^* = 2\pi\frac{\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c}}{\mathbf{a}\cdot(\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c})}, \quad \mathbf{b}^* = 2\pi\frac{\mathbf{c}\times\mathbf{a}}{\mathbf{a}\cdot(\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c})}, \quad \mathbf{c}^* = 2\pi\frac{\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}}{\mathbf{a}\cdot(\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c})} \quad \text{(2.18)}$$

In a monoclinic crystal ($\beta \neq 90^\circ$, Figs. 2.8, 2.9), $\mathbf{a}^*$ and $\mathbf{a}$ are not parallel: $|\mathbf{a}^*| = 1/(a\cos\theta)$ where $\theta = \beta - 90^\circ$.
